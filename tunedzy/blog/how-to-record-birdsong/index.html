How to Record Birdsong: Patience, Placement and Respect

Updated October 2026 · Field recording and nature sounds

Quick answer

Recording birdsong rewards patience. Find a place where birds are active, arrive early, keep still and place the microphone where it can hear the birds without the traffic or wind dominating. Record long sessions, keep your distance, and never disturb nests or change the birds’ behaviour.

Birdsong is one of the most beautiful sounds in nature, and one of the most rewarding to record. A good recording can capture the dawn chorus, the call of a single species or the subtle chatter of a flock. It takes patience, careful placement and respect for the birds, but the results can be remarkable, and they can enrich videos, music and personal archives.

This post covers choosing a place and time, placing the microphone, setting the level and keeping the birds undisturbed.

Choose the place and the time

Birds are most active early in the morning, and again in the evening, so the dawn chorus is often the best time to record. Choose a place with trees, shrubs or water, where birds gather. A quiet park, a woodland edge or a garden with feeders can all be good. Visit the place several times before recording, and learn when the birds are most active.

Arrive early and keep still

Arrive before the birds become active, and set up quietly. Movement and voices scare birds away, so keep still once you have settled. Many experienced recordists wait for twenty minutes or more before the birds return to their normal behaviour. Patience is the most important skill in birdsong recording.

Place the microphone

Place the microphone where it can hear the birds clearly, usually a few metres from the trees they use. Too close can disturb the birds and capture every rustle, while too far can lose the detail. Test a few positions by listening on headphones, and move the microphone slowly to find the sweet spot.

Handle the wind and the background

Wind and distant traffic are the main problems. Use a windscreen, and choose a sheltered position if possible. Record early in the morning, when traffic is light, and note the background sounds that you cannot avoid. A recording with some background can still be beautiful, as long as the birds are clear.

Set the level

Birds can be quiet and then suddenly loud, and a close call can overload the recording. Set the level with a short test, leaving headroom for the loudest song. Use the peak meter, and watch for clipping during the first few minutes of activity.

Record long sessions

Birds do not sing on demand, and a long session gives you the best chance of capturing a full range of calls. Record for an hour or more, and choose the best moments later. Keep the battery and storage in mind, and check the recorder at intervals.

Respect the birds

Keep a respectful distance, especially during nesting season. Do not approach a nest, and do not play recordings of other birds to attract the birds you want, since this can change their behaviour and stress them. If a bird stops singing or moves away because of you, step back and wait. A recording that harms a bird is not worth making.

Identify the species

Note the species you hear, if you can identify them. A short note with the date, the time and the place adds value to the recording, especially if it is used in a video or an archive. Local birdwatching groups and identification apps can help, and you can learn the calls over time.

Common mistakes

  • Arriving late, after the best activity has passed.
  • Moving about and frightening the birds away.
  • Recording too close to a nest, and disturbing the birds.
  • Playing other birds’ calls to attract the birds, which can harm them.
  • Recording without a windscreen, so the wind spoils the take.

A first birdsong session

  1. Visit the place once, at the time you plan to record, and listen.
  2. Arrive early, and set up quietly.
  3. Test the microphone position on headphones, and set the level.
  4. Record for an hour with a windscreen in place.
  5. Label the file with the date, the time, the place and any species you identified.

Log what you hear

Keep a notebook or a simple file alongside the recordings. Note the time, the weather, the species you think you hear and the moments you are unsure of. Birdsong is easy to mistake, and an honest log means you can revisit uncertain identifications later, with a field guide or a knowledgeable local recordist.

Limits

Birdsong recording depends on the season, the weather and the local birds. Some days will be quiet, and some species may be rare. The aim is to capture the place honestly, and to respect the birds throughout. A quiet session is still a session, and it teaches you the place.

Frequently asked questions

When is the best time to record birdsong?

Early morning, around dawn, when birds are most active.

How close should I put the microphone?

Close enough to hear the birds clearly, but not so close that you disturb them or their nests.

Can I play other birds to attract them?

It is better not to. It can change behaviour and stress the birds.

Do I need to identify the species?

It adds value to the recording, but a recording without identification is still useful.
