Audio Editing Shortcuts Explained: Working Faster Without Losing Care

Updated October 2026 · Editing audio well

Quick answer

Audio editing shortcuts speed up cutting, zooming, playback and navigation. Learn a small set in a fixed order, starting with play and stop, zoom, cut, undo and marker placement. Speed is useful, but careful listening should not be sacrificed for it, because the fastest edit is the one that does not need to be redone.

Editing is repetitive, and a few seconds saved on each action add up to hours over a project. Keyboard shortcuts are the most direct way to save that time. They let the hands stay on the keyboard, while the eyes stay on the waveform and the ears stay on the sound. Learning shortcuts is worth the effort, but it should be done in a sensible order, and speed should never replace careful listening.

This post explains the kinds of shortcuts that matter most, how to learn them gradually and how to stay careful while working quickly. The exact keys differ between editors, so check your own editor’s reference for the specifics.

Start with playback and navigation

The first shortcuts to learn are the ones you use most: play and stop, jumping to the start and end, moving forward and back by a set amount, and looping a section. These let you listen the way you need to, without reaching for the mouse. Learn these before anything else, because they are used constantly.

Learn zoom

Zooming in and out is essential for precise work. Learn the shortcut for zooming to the selection and for returning to the full view. Zooming to a join or a click is how you make small, accurate changes, so it is worth practising until it feels natural.

Learn the cut and the selection

Cutting, copying, pasting and selecting are the core of the edit. Learn the shortcut for splitting a clip at the playhead, for deleting a selection and for selecting the next region. These let you work through a recording quickly, cut by cut.

Learn undo and redo

Undo is the most important shortcut of all. Learn it early, and use it freely while experimenting. A fast editor who undoes mistakes quickly makes more careful decisions than one who is afraid to try a change. Make sure you know how far the undo history goes in your editor.

Learn markers

Placing a marker with a single key press is one of the most useful habits. As you listen, you can mark problems and keepers without stopping, which speeds up the first listen and the structural edit. The markers post in this series explains how to use them well.

Build the set in order

  1. Learn play, stop and navigation first.
  2. Add zoom, so you can work precisely.
  3. Add cut, select and delete for the structural work.
  4. Add undo and redo, and use them freely.
  5. Add markers, then any other shortcuts you use often.

Stay careful while working fast

Speed is a means to an end, and the end is a clean, well-judged recording. A fast cut that clips a consonant or leaves a click has to be fixed later, and the time saved disappears. Slow down at the joins, listen to the result at normal speed, and use the shortcuts to make the work smoother rather than rushed.

Common mistakes

  • Learning many shortcuts at once, and remembering none.
  • Using fast cuts without listening to the join.
  • Being afraid to undo, which leads to over-cautious edits.
  • Working at a single zoom level throughout.
  • Relying on shortcuts without checking the final result by ear.

Make a one-page shortcut sheet

Write down the ten shortcuts you use most, and keep the sheet beside your screen for the first few weeks. Add one new shortcut each time you notice yourself reaching for the mouse to do something repeatable. A short, steady list is far easier to learn than a long reference card you never open.

Practise on a short file

Choose a clip of a minute or two and edit it using only the keyboard. Time yourself the first time, and again the next day. The aim is not speed for its own sake. It is to make the basic moves automatic, so your attention stays on the sound rather than on the menus.

Using a template for repeat jobs

If you repeat the same jobs for every file, such as trimming the opening, applying a level change and exporting in one format, save those steps as a template or a macro if your editor allows it. A template removes the small decisions that slow you down and invites fewer mistakes. Test it on a copy first, and check the first few results by ear, since a template that worked on one kind of recording may not suit another. Review it every few months, because your equipment and your files change over time.

Limits

Shortcuts differ between editors, and some features have no default key. Check your editor’s reference, and consider customising the keys you use most. The principles are the same across editors, and the speed comes from practice rather than from memorising a long list.

Check the result carefully

Speed should not reduce the quality of the final file. The Vocal Checker shows clipping, noise and level problems in a recording you own, and the Tunedzy Go page covers cleaning phone recordings. For the order in which these actions belong, read the editing workflow post in this series.

Frequently asked questions

Which shortcuts should I learn first?

Play, stop and navigation, then zoom, cut and undo.

Are shortcuts the same in every editor?

No. The keys differ, but the actions are similar. Check your editor’s reference.

Does working fast reduce quality?

It can, if joins are not listened to. Slow down at the joins and check the result.

Should I customise my shortcuts?

Yes, for the actions you use most often, once you know the defaults.
