How to Match Levels Between Clips and Recordings

Updated October 2026 · Levels and metering explained

Quick answer

Match levels between clips by choosing a shared reference, setting each clip to that reference by ear and by meter, and then checking the joins. Use the average level for balance and the peaks for safety. A final check of the whole sequence catches the differences that a single clip hides.

When several clips or recordings are put together, their levels rarely agree. One interview is quiet, another is loud, and a voice-over recorded on a different day sits lower again. Viewers and listeners notice these jumps, and they reach for the volume control each time. Matching levels is therefore one of the most useful jobs in finishing a project, and it is a skill that improves with practice.

This post explains how to choose a reference, how to match each clip by ear and by meter, how to check the joins and how to keep the matching consistent for future work.

Choose a shared reference

Start by choosing a reference level that the whole project will use. A good reference is the level of the cleanest, most typical clip, because it shows how the speech should sound in the finished work. Play that clip at a comfortable listening level, and note its average level and its peaks. Those numbers become the target for every other clip.

Writing the reference in the project notes is essential. A reference that exists only in memory will drift over a long project, and the later clips will not match the earlier ones.

Match by ear first

Listening is the best guide, because meters can disagree with what the ear hears. Play each clip in turn, comparing it with the reference, and adjust its gain until the speech sits at the same apparent level. Listen to the whole clip, not just a loud word, and pay attention to the quiet passages. A clip that sounds even but is quiet overall may need more gain than a clip that has a loud word.

Confirm with the meter

Once the clips sound matched, confirm the averages on a meter. A large difference in the average level points to a clip that needs a small correction, even if the ear was satisfied. Use the peak meter to check that the adjustment has not pushed any word into clipping. Small corrections are usually enough, and large ones are a sign that the source differs too much.

Check the joins

A level match can still sound wrong at the join if the room tone differs. Play each join at normal speed, and listen for a change in background sound, not only in volume. A short crossfade or a matched piece of room tone can smooth the change. The crossfade guide explains the method.

Check the whole sequence

After matching each clip, play the whole sequence from start to finish, without stopping. Problems that are invisible in a single clip can become obvious in the flow of the project. Note any clip that stands out, and return to it for a small adjustment.

Keep the matching consistent

Use the same reference for future projects, so each series sounds like one show. Record the reference level in your template, and check new recordings against it before editing. A consistent reference saves time on every project, and it is one of the simplest ways to make your work sound professional.

Working with clips from different sources

Clips that come from different sources need more care than clips from the same session. A clip from a phone, a clip from a studio microphone and a clip from a laptop may all be well made, but they will carry different tonal character and different noise. Matching the level is only the first step. Listen for differences in brightness and warmth, and consider a light, matched EQ on the clip that differs most, so the sequence sounds like one voice in one place. Save the EQ settings with the clip, so the match can be repeated if the clip is re-exported later. Keep the settings close to the reference note, so the match is easy to find when the project is revisited months later.

When the sources differ a great deal, it can help to place the most distinctive clip where the change will feel intentional, such as at the start of a new section. A deliberate change of place is easier for listeners to accept than an unexplained one in the middle of a conversation.

Limits

Matching levels cannot fix recordings that differ in tone, distance or room. A clip recorded far from the microphone will not match a close one, however carefully the level is set. In those cases, the better fix is to re-record, or to accept that the difference exists and place the clips where it will be least noticed.

Frequently asked questions

What should I use as the reference?

The cleanest, most typical clip in the project, measured for average level and peaks.

Should I match by ear or by meter?

Both. The ear decides, and the meter confirms the average and checks the peaks.

Why do matched clips still sound different?

Their tone, distance or room differs. Matching levels cannot fix those differences.

How do I keep matching consistent over time?

Record the reference in your template, and check every new recording against it.
