Stopping Throat Clearing on Recordings: Habits That Help the Take

Updated October 2026 · Voice care for speakers and singers

Quick answer

Throat clearing is a common habit that irritates the vocal folds and ruins takes. It often comes from dryness, tension or nerves. Reduce it by sipping water between takes, relaxing the throat, using a gentle swallow instead of a cough, and recording after a short pause. If the need to clear the throat is constant, see a doctor.

Throat clearing is one of the most common interruptions in a recording. A small cough or a hard clear appears just before a line, and the take must be repeated. The habit is also hard on the voice, because forcing air and mucus against the vocal folds irritates them. Stopping throat clearing protects the voice and saves time in the studio, and it can be learned with simple changes.

This post explains why the habit happens, how it harms the voice and the take and the practical steps that reduce it.

Why people clear their throats

Throat clearing often comes from a feeling of something in the throat, which may be mucus, dryness or tension. Nerves can make the throat feel tight, and the body responds by trying to clear it. Sometimes the habit is simply automatic, triggered by the start of a recording. Understanding the cause helps you choose the right change.

How it harms the voice

A hard clear forces air against the vocal folds, and it can bring the folds together with force. Repeated clearing irritates the lining of the throat, makes the voice rougher and can lead to swelling over time. It also tires the voice, which then has less stamina for the rest of the session.

How it ruins the take

A throat clear is a loud, unwanted sound, and it is hard to remove afterwards. It often lands right before the line the speaker wanted to deliver, which means the take must be repeated. Each repeat costs time and energy, and it can make the speaker more nervous, which causes more clearing.

Sip water between takes

Sip water between takes, not during them. A few sips keep the throat moist and reduce the feeling of dryness that triggers the habit. Keep the glass away from the microphone, and avoid drinking anything that could cause a click or a gulp in the recording.

Replace the clear with a swallow

A gentle swallow often clears the throat without the damage of a hard clear. Swallow slowly, and let the throat settle. If the feeling persists, a few gentle hums can help before you speak. Practise the swallow when you are not recording, so it becomes the habit.

Relax the throat and the breath

Tension in the throat and neck can cause the feeling of something stuck. Relax the jaw, drop the shoulders and take a slow breath before each take. A relaxed breath makes the throat feel more open and reduces the need to clear it.

Record after a short pause

Pause for a few seconds before starting a take, so the throat has time to settle. Take a breath, swallow gently and then begin. A short pause at the start of each take gives the throat its moment, and it often prevents the clear altogether.

When it is constant

If the need to clear the throat is constant, or comes with pain, a cough that does not stop, or a change in the voice, see a doctor. Persistent throat symptoms can have causes that need treatment, such as reflux, allergy or infection, and a doctor can help identify them.

Common mistakes

  • Clearing the throat hard before every take.
  • Drinking during a take, which can cause clicks or gulps.
  • Starting a take straight after speaking, without a pause.
  • Ignoring a persistent need to clear the throat.
  • Using throat sprays or lozenges as a quick fix before each take.

A simple routine

  1. Sip water between takes, away from the microphone.
  2. Relax the jaw and shoulders, and take a slow breath.
  3. Swallow gently instead of clearing hard.
  4. Pause for a few seconds before each take.
  5. See a doctor if the need to clear the throat persists.

Noticing the trigger

Most throat clearing starts with a feeling, such as tickle, dryness or tension at the start of a sentence. Notice when the urge arrives. Often the trigger is a tense breath or a rushed start. A slower breath before the first word, and a relaxed jaw, can stop the urge before it turns into a sound.

Plan takes around the urge

If you know a passage is likely to set off a clear, pause before it and take a fresh breath. Mark the spot in the script, so you can take the same care on the next take. Planning the pauses turns a random interruption into a predictable part of the session.

Limits

These steps reduce the habit in many people, but not all. The cause varies, and some causes need medical care. General advice cannot replace an assessment when the symptom is persistent or painful.

Frequently asked questions

Is throat clearing always bad?

A gentle clear is fine occasionally, but hard, repeated clearing irritates the folds.

Should I drink during a take?

Better not. Sip between takes, away from the microphone, to avoid clicks.

What is a better alternative to clearing?

A gentle swallow, a relaxed breath and a short pause before the take.

When should I see a doctor?

If the need to clear the throat is constant, or comes with pain, a persistent cough or a voice change.
