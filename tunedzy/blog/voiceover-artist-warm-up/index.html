A Voiceover Artist Warm-Up: Preparing the Voice for Recording

Updated October 2026 · Voice care for speakers and singers

Quick answer

A voiceover warm-up prepares the breath, the voice and the articulation before recording. A short routine of breathing, gentle humming, lip and tongue exercises and a few slow phrases at the energy of the script gets the voice ready. Keep the warm-up consistent so the voice settles quickly each session.

Voiceover work asks the voice to be clear, expressive and consistent across many takes. A voice that is cold or tight at the start of a session often sounds stiff, and the first takes may need repeating. A short warm-up before recording prepares the breath, the voice and the articulation, so the first take sounds as natural as the last. The routine is brief, and it pays off in every session.

This post sets out a simple warm-up for voiceover artists and other recording speakers, with notes on pacing, energy and the signs that the voice is ready.

Why warm up for voiceover

Voiceover demands precision, and the voice must be ready to deliver clear words quickly and consistently. A cold start can make the voice sound tight, the consonants soft and the pitch unsettled. A warm-up brings the voice to a comfortable working state, so the performance can begin with confidence and the takes stay consistent.

Start with breath

Begin with slow breaths, low in the body, letting the stomach and ribs move gently. Breathe in for four counts and out for six or more, several times. Notice whether the shoulders stay relaxed. Good breath support is the foundation of steady speech, and it is the first thing to settle.

Add gentle humming

Humming on a comfortable note wakes the voice gently. Start quietly, and move slowly up and down a small range. Let the hum vibrate in the lips and the face, which helps the voice feel connected and resonant. Keep the volume soft, since the aim is to prepare the voice, not to test it.

Articulation exercises

Clear articulation is a central skill for voiceover. Practise lip, tongue and jaw movements with simple exercises: say a range of consonants clearly, roll a gentle R, or repeat a short tongue-twister slowly, then at normal speed. Keep the jaw loose and the movements precise, and notice any sound that feels stiff or lazy.

Read a few lines aloud

Finish the warm-up by reading a few lines from the script, at the energy and pace you plan to use. This connects the warm-up to the work and shows whether the voice is ready. Read slowly first, then at the target pace, and adjust the energy until the reading feels natural.

Keep the warm-up short and consistent

A warm-up of about ten minutes is enough for most sessions. Keep it consistent, so the voice learns the routine and settles quickly. A familiar warm-up also reduces anxiety, which helps the voice stay relaxed during recording.

Check the warm-up on a recording

Record a short test at the end of the warm-up, and listen on headphones. Check for tightness, uneven breath and soft consonants. If something sounds off, extend the warm-up a little before starting the session. The test recording is a useful mirror for the voice.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the warm-up because the session feels urgent.
  • Starting at full volume or full energy without preparation.
  • Rushing the breath, so the voice runs out of support.
  • Over-articulating in the warm-up, which can strain the jaw.
  • Ignoring the signs of tension in the neck and shoulders.

A simple warm-up routine

  1. Take six slow breaths, low in the body.
  2. Hum gently on a comfortable note, moving slowly through a small range.
  3. Practise clear consonants and gentle lip and tongue movements.
  4. Read a few script lines slowly, then at the target pace.
  5. Record a short test, and check the sound before starting.

Warm up at the same time each session

A routine at the same point in the day, before the first take, becomes a cue that the voice is about to work. Over time the warm-up feels automatic, and the first take of the day stops being the rough one. Keep the same order each time, even when you are short of time, and shorten the routine rather than skipping it.

Stop when the warm-up feels effortful

A warm-up should loosen the voice, not tire it. If a sound feels forced, scale back the exercise, drink some water and try again. If the effort persists, leave the warm-up and move on to lighter reading. Pushing through a warm-up that feels wrong usually makes the first session worse, not better.

Limits

Warm-up routines vary between artists, and what suits one voice may not suit another. Adapt the routine to your own voice, and stop any exercise that causes strain. A voice teacher or a speech and language therapist can help you build a routine that fits.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a voiceover warm-up take?

About ten minutes is enough for most sessions, kept consistent each time.

Should I read the script in the warm-up?

Yes, a few lines at the target energy show whether the voice is ready.

What if my voice still feels stiff?

Extend the warm-up gently, and check with a short test recording.

Are tongue twisters useful?

Yes, if practised slowly and clearly, then at speed. Stop if the jaw feels strained.
