Acoustic Panels vs Blankets: What Each One Does for a Home Studio

Updated October 2026 · Planning a home studio

Quick answer

Acoustic panels and blankets both absorb sound, and both can reduce echo in a home studio. Panels are more predictable and suit fixed positions, while blankets are cheaper, flexible and easy to move. Choose by how permanent the setup is, how much space you have and how much control you need, and place either at the reflection points first.

Two of the most common ways to soften a home studio are acoustic panels and soft blankets. Both absorb sound, and both can reduce the echo that makes a voice sound distant. They differ in cost, appearance, consistency and how easily they can be moved. Choosing between them depends on the room, the budget and how permanent the studio will be. This post compares them honestly.

Both work on the same principle: soft, porous material absorbs energy from sound waves that reach it, reducing the reflections that bounce around the room. The effectiveness depends more on where they are placed and how much area they cover than on the type of material alone.

How panels work

Acoustic panels are rigid or semi-rigid units made from dense absorbent material, often covered in fabric. They are designed to absorb sound across a useful range of frequencies, and they hold their shape on the wall. Their performance is more predictable than a blanket, because the material and the thickness are known.

How blankets work

Blankets, such as moving blankets, thick duvets or heavy throws, absorb sound through their soft fibres. They are cheaper, widely available and easy to move. Their performance is less predictable, because the thickness, the weave and how they are hung all change the result. Blankets work best when they are hung with a gap behind them, which improves their absorption, especially at lower frequencies.

Where each works best

  • Panels: fixed positions, such as the reflection points near a microphone, where a permanent, predictable result is wanted.
  • Blankets: flexible positions, temporary setups, rooms that change use and a tight budget.
  • Either: behind the microphone and beside the speaker, where the first reflections arrive.

Cost and appearance

Blankets cost little and can be hidden or removed easily. Panels cost more, and they are a visible part of the room, although many are designed to look neat. If the room is shared with others, the appearance of the treatment may matter as much as its effect. Choose what you are happy to live with for the years you will use the room.

Placement matters more

A small number of well-placed panels or blankets can do more than a large number placed at random. Start with the reflection points near the microphone, and then add material across the room. Test each change with a clap and a short recording, and keep the placement that gives the clearest voice.

Mounting and spacing

Mounting changes the result. A panel mounted flat against a wall absorbs differently from one held off the wall with a gap behind it. The gap lets more of the low frequencies reach the material, which matters in boomy rooms. Use the same mounting for a fair comparison, and avoid changing two things at once when you test.

Spacing matters too. Two pieces placed at the reflection points near a microphone will often do more than several placed at random across a wall. Measure those points before you buy, and keep the spacing the same when you move a piece to test another position.

Keeping the treatment in use

Treatment has to fit the way you work. If a panel or a blanket gets in the way of a door, a window or a desk drawer, it will soon be moved or removed. Choose positions you can maintain. Blankets need checking for dust and sagging, and panels need their fixings checked from time to time. A plan that survives daily life is worth more than an ideal one that comes down after a month.

Hanging a blanket safely

A blanket hung on a wall needs a secure, safe fixing that will not fall or damage the wall. Use proper hooks or a rail, and check the fixing regularly. Keep the blanket away from heat sources, since a hot lamp or a radiator can be a fire risk. A simple mounting that is safe and easy to remove is better than a neat one that is hard to maintain.

Limits

Neither panels nor blankets solve every acoustic problem. Blankets in particular vary widely in effect, and claims about any product should be tested in your own room. Very low-frequency problems need a different approach, and no soft material fully removes the room’s character. Judge the result by listening.

A simple choice

  1. Start with blankets at the reflection points, and test the change.
  2. If the change helps and the setup is permanent, consider panels at the same points.
  3. Add material across the room if the echo tail is still long.
  4. Keep the arrangement you can live with, and test it regularly.

Frequently asked questions

Are panels better than blankets?

Panels are more predictable, but blankets can work well at a lower cost, especially in a flexible setup.

Where should I place them first?

At the reflection points near the microphone and the speaker.

Do blankets need a gap behind them?

A gap improves their absorption, especially at lower frequencies.

Is one enough?

Often a few well-placed pieces help more than many placed at random. Test each change.
