How to Add Podcast Chapters for Video and Audio Episodes

Updated October 2026 · Video podcasts and audio-first shows

Quick answer

Add chapters at the points where the topic changes, give each one a clear title that names the subject, and check any automatic chapters against the episode. Consistent chapters help listeners jump to what they want, and they make clips and show notes easier to write.

Chapters divide an episode into named sections, so listeners can see what is coming and jump to the part they came for. They have become more important as shows move between audio and video, and as listeners skip between episodes. A well-chaptered show is easier to navigate, easier to clip and easier to recommend.

This guide covers when chapters are worth the effort, how to write titles that help, how to check automatic suggestions, and how to keep the habit across a season.

When chapters help

Chapters help most in long episodes and in episodes with several distinct topics. An interview with three clear stages, or a solo episode that covers a process, benefits from chapters. A short episode that makes one point may not need them. Ask whether a listener would want to skip to a specific part, and add chapters where the answer is yes.

Chapters also help the people who do not hear the whole episode, such as those who search your show’s notes for a particular subject.

Write clear titles

A good chapter title names the topic in plain words. “Why the first draft failed” tells a listener more than “Part two”. Keep titles short, usually under six words, and make them consistent in style across the episode. Avoid jokes in titles if the joke is not clear without the context.

Write the titles as the listener would search for them. A title that matches the words a listener would type is more useful than one that only the host understands.

Use the transcript to find the breaks

The transcript shows where the conversation changes direction. Read it once and mark each point where the topic moves on. Those marks are your chapter starts. A transcript also shows the exact words to use in the chapter title.

Chapters placed at the start of a topic, not in the middle of a sentence, feel natural. Check each start in the audio before publishing, because a transcript can be a few seconds out.

Check automatic chapters

Some phone systems and editing tools now suggest chapters automatically. Apple has added automatic chapter generation on recent versions of its software, and reports describe it as a way to make episodes easier to navigate. Automatic chapters are a starting point. They can mislabel a section, miss a turn in the conversation or create a chapter where none is needed.

Listen to each suggested chapter, check its start point and rename any title that is unclear. Remove chapters that add no value. A short list of accurate chapters beats a long list of vague ones.

Keep the chapters consistent

Use the same rules across the season. Choose a maximum number of chapters for each episode type, and use the same style of title. Consistent chapters let listeners learn how your show is organised, and they make it easier to compare episodes.

Keep the chapter list in the show notes too. Listeners who read the notes before listening can see the structure of the episode, which helps them decide.

Chapters for video

In a video episode, chapters can also point to a visual moment, such as a demonstration or a guest’s answer to a question. Name the chapter so an audio listener understands it, even if they cannot see the picture. Describe the visual in words where it matters, so the chapter makes sense without the picture.

Use chapters to plan the next episode

Chapter titles reveal how a conversation is structured, and that structure can guide the next episode. If several chapters across a season are about the same question, the audience may want a whole episode on it. If a chapter always runs long and listeners skip it, the topic may need a tighter treatment. Review chapter lengths and titles each month alongside your completion data, and let them suggest the next topic.

Chapters also help the show notes. A list of chapter titles with times is a summary listeners can scan, and it can be reused in social posts.

Give each chapter a single job

A good chapter does one thing: it introduces a topic, tells a story, answers a question or marks a turn. A chapter that tries to do several things confuses the listener about where to go. If a section holds two distinct topics, split it into two chapters with clear titles.

Keep the number of chapters proportional to the episode. A thirty-minute show with twenty chapters is harder to use than one with six clear ones.

Limits

Chapters only help if they are accurate. A chapter that points to the wrong place frustrates listeners more than having no chapters at all. Check them after every episode, especially when you use automatic tools, and treat them as part of the editing process rather than an afterthought.

Check the episode on a phone

Once the chapters are in, play the episode on a phone and jump between them. Check that each one starts cleanly and that the level is steady across the section. Frequently asked questions

Do all episodes need chapters?

No. Add them to longer episodes or those with several distinct topics.

Can I trust automatic chapters?

Only after checking. They can mislabel sections or miss turns in the conversation.

How many chapters should an episode have?

As many as the topics need, and no more. Five to ten is common for an hour-long episode, but there is no fixed rule.

Where do chapters appear?

That depends on the platform. Check the platform’s help page for how it displays and uses them.
