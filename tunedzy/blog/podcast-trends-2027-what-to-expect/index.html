Podcast Trends to Expect in 2027 and What to Do About Them

Updated October 2026 · Podcast trends and what changes next

Quick answer

Expect podcasts to look more like video, to use AI for routine production, and to be judged on how often listeners return. Small shows can prepare by keeping a clean audio master, choosing one video format they can repeat, and tracking a few retention numbers.

Predicting podcast trends is mostly a matter of reading what platforms announce and what creators already do. Two directions are clear as of October 2026. Video is becoming a normal way to watch a show, and the tools for making shows are getting faster. Neither trend means audio is finished. It means audio has to work in more places, and a small show has to plan for that without a large team.

This post separates what has been announced from what is only an expectation. The announcements are real, and you can plan around them. The expectations are reasoned guesses, and they may be wrong. The practical steps at the end are useful either way, because they make a show easier to adapt when the platforms change.

Video keeps moving closer to audio

Apple announced video podcasts in February 2026, with listeners able to switch between watching and listening in the same episode. Apple has also said the feature is coming to Mac and Apple TV, with a shipping timeline it describes as autumn 2026. Reports differ on the exact release, so check Apple’s own newsroom before you plan around a date. The direction is the point. A listener may start a show on a phone, watch part of it on a television, and switch to audio on a commute. A show that exists only as audio loses some of those listeners by design, and a show that exists only as video loses the ones who cannot watch.

What this means in practice is that the episode has to hold up in both forms. Spoken content must carry the point on its own, and visual content must be explained in words for the listener who has switched to audio. A small team does not need to film everything. It needs to decide, for each episode, which parts are visual and which are not.

AI will handle more of the routine work

Expect transcription, first-pass cleanup, chapter markers and show notes to be automated in most editing tools. Some phone systems already produce chapters and transcripts on the device, and several editing programs offer automatic transcripts. The routine work that used to take an evening now takes minutes, and that changes what a small show can afford to do.

The human work moves toward decisions: what to cut, what the episode is for, and whether the sound still feels like your show. A show that is easy to produce is also easy to copy, so the voice, the consistency and the choice of topics become the real differences. A tool can tidy a conversation. It cannot tell you that the best moment is the one you almost removed.

Measurement will favour return visits

Downloads and reach are noisy numbers. They count clicks, automated requests and people who never pressed play. Expect more attention on whether people finish episodes and come back for the next one. Hosting dashboards already show drop-off, and a steady audience that listens to most of each episode is worth more than a spike from one viral clip.

Start tracking completion rate and returning listeners now, even if your platform shows them only roughly. Compare episodes on similar topics, because a tutorial and a conversation behave differently. Over a season, those comparisons tell you more about your audience than any single chart position.

Branded shows keep growing

Businesses are using podcasts as a content channel, and that raises the bar for production. A branded show competes for attention with independent shows that have a clear personality. The lesson for small creators is that a focused topic and a recognisable host still matter more than a large budget. A branded show with a loud sponsor and a flat host will lose to a plain show with a host people trust.

Sponsorship is also changing. Host-read advertising remains common, and advertisers increasingly ask for tracking codes and clear results. A small show can take advantage of that by offering sponsors a specific audience and a measurable call to action, rather than a general reach figure.

Platforms will keep adding listening modes

Several platforms are testing ways to make a player more suited to speech: larger controls, optional audio-first views and playback that adjusts to the speaker. One report describes a YouTube mode that replaces the picture with a still image and is limited to Premium subscribers. Treat such details as reported until you see the platform’s own help page, because features change often.

The useful habit is to keep your audio sounding right in every mode. A player that speeds up slow passages, or that shows only a still image, still plays your sound. If the level is steady and the speech is clear, the feature works for you rather than against you.

What to do this year

  1. Keep a clean, lossless master of every episode, so you can make a video or a short clip later without re-recording.
  2. Choose one video format you can repeat, such as a fixed camera and a simple title card, rather than many experiments.
  3. Write down your episode length, release day and loudness target, and keep them steady across the season.
  4. Track completion rate and returning listeners each month, and compare episodes on the same topic.
  5. Test your audio on a phone speaker before every release, because many listeners still start there.

Where these predictions could be wrong

A platform can change its plans quickly, and a feature announced today can ship late or change shape. Regulation can also change the rules for voices and for labelling AI-generated speech, which affects how shows are made. Treat this post as a set of expectations, not a schedule.

The safe habits are the same whatever happens: good audio, a steady format and honest measurement. They also make a later move to video easier, because the audio master is already in order and the rhythm of the show is already set.

Frequently asked questions

Will audio podcasts disappear?

No. Audio remains a strong format, especially for commuting and long listens. The change is that many shows will also have a video version.

Should I start a video podcast now?

Only if you can repeat it. A simple, steady video version is better than an irregular one.

What is the most useful number to track?

Completion rate and returning listeners. They show whether people value the show, not only whether they clicked.

Do I need AI tools to keep up?

Not to start. They save time on routine tasks, but your choices about content and sound still decide the result.
