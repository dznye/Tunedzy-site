Meeting Recording and Transcription: Consent and Good Practice

Updated October 2026 · Speech recognition and on-device transcription

Quick answer

Recording and transcribing a meeting requires consent in many places, and the rules vary by country and by the people present. Tell everyone before recording, say what the recording and transcript will be used for, store them securely and delete them when they are no longer needed.

Meeting recordings and automatic transcripts have become common in offices, classrooms and remote teams. They save time and make it easier to share decisions, but they also raise questions about privacy and consent. A recording that captures a candid discussion can be sensitive, and a transcript can be searched, copied and shared far more easily than a conversation was ever shared before.

This post covers the practical side: when consent is needed, what to tell people, how to handle the files and the mistakes that cause trouble. It is general guidance, not legal advice, and the rules differ between places and workplaces.

Why consent matters

A recording captures what people said, in their own voices, and a transcript captures the words in a searchable form. Many people are comfortable in a meeting but not comfortable being recorded or transcribed. Consent respects that difference, and it protects the organisation as well as the people in the room. In many places, recording a conversation without the consent of the people involved is not allowed, and it can damage trust even where it is.

The safest practice is to ask every participant before the recording starts, and to make it easy to say no without consequence.

What to tell attendees

Say that the meeting is being recorded and transcribed, who will have access to the files, what they will be used for and how long they will be kept. Explain whether an automatic tool will process the audio, and whether the audio or transcript leaves the organisation. A short written note in the invitation helps, and a spoken confirmation at the start of the meeting makes the consent clear.

Offer an alternative, such as a manual note-taker, for anyone who does not want to be recorded. Respect that choice.

Check the tool before you use it

Before you use a meeting recording or transcription tool, read its privacy terms. Check whether the audio is stored, for how long, whether it is used to train models, and where it is processed. A tool that processes on the device, or that deletes files quickly and clearly, is easier to justify than one that keeps everything. The privacy post in this series sets out the questions to ask.

Keep the tool’s terms with your records, so you can show what was agreed.

Store and share with care

Store recordings and transcripts in a secure place with limited access. Share them only with people who need them, and avoid sending them by open links that anyone can use. Consider setting an expiry date for the files, and delete them when the purpose has been met, unless a rule requires you to keep them.

A transcript is easy to copy, so mark it as confidential where appropriate, and think about what would happen if it were shared more widely than intended.

Common mistakes

  • Recording before asking, or assuming that a participant has agreed by staying in the room.
  • Using a tool without reading its terms about storage and training.
  • Keeping recordings indefinitely, long after the meeting has served its purpose.
  • Sharing transcripts with people outside the meeting who were never meant to see them.
  • Forgetting that a transcript can include personal or sensitive details that were said in passing.

When to avoid recording

Some meetings should not be recorded at all, such as those that discuss individual performance, health, legal advice or confidential matters that need strict control. In those cases, a short written summary, agreed by the participants, may be a better record than an automatic transcript.

Keep the record of consent

When consent is given, record it in a way you can show later. A written email, a message in the meeting invitation or a recorded spoken confirmation at the start all work, provided they say what was agreed. Store the record with the recording, so the two stay together and nobody has to search for the proof of consent.

If someone withdraws consent during the meeting, stop recording straight away, and note the time. Delete the part that was made without consent, and tell the people involved what you have done.

Limits

The rules on recording and transcription vary by country, sector and workplace policy, and they change over time. This post describes good practice rather than the law of any place. For a workplace policy, consult the people responsible for data protection and employment, and take advice where needed.

Keep the recording clear and fair

Frequently asked questions

Do I need consent to record a meeting?

In many places, yes, from everyone present. Check the rules where you are and tell attendees before recording.

Can I rely on the default settings of a tool?

Check them. Defaults may keep audio longer or use it for training.

How long should I keep a transcript?

Only as long as the purpose requires, unless a rule says otherwise. Set an expiry date.

What if someone refuses to be recorded?

Respect the choice and offer another way to take notes.
