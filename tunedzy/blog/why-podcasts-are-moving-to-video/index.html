Why Podcasts Are Moving to Video and What It Means for Audio

Updated October 2026 · Podcast trends and what changes next

Quick answer

Podcasts are moving to video because platforms now show video alongside audio, and viewers can switch between them. Audio still matters, but the sound has to work in both formats. Add video where it helps the story, and keep the audio master as the foundation.

For years, video and audio podcasts were treated as separate projects. Now many platforms let the same episode be watched and heard, and some shows are recorded with video from the start. The change is less about a new audience and more about how people choose to consume the same conversation. Audio does not disappear, but it has to fit into a bigger picture.

For a podcaster, the question is practical. Does video help this show? If so, how do you add it without damaging the sound that the show is known for? The sections below work through the reasons for the shift, the trade-offs and a simple path for trying it.

What is driving the change

Apple announced video podcasts in February 2026, with the ability to switch between video and audio and new routes for video advertising. Other platforms are adding similar features, and some have tested audio-first modes that treat the player differently. The common thread is that platforms want the show to be watchable and listenable without a break in the episode.

Viewers have also shown they will watch conversations, especially when the people on screen react to each other. A recorded conversation with visible expressions and a shared object on the table gives the viewer something the audio cannot. That is the reason video can work, when the content gives it something to show.

What viewers expect

Viewers expect a picture that matches the conversation: the right framing, good lighting and a camera that does not drift. They also expect the sound to be as good as on an audio show. Many viewers judge a video show by its sound first. Poor audio spoils a good picture faster than the reverse, because people will stop watching a dim, blurry show less readily than they will stop listening to a harsh one.

Viewers also expect the video to be worth watching. A static camera on a host who never moves can be fine, but only if the conversation is strong. Video does not rescue a weak episode.

The production trade-offs

Video adds work: cameras, lighting, framing, and editing for cuts. It also adds a second master, a second export and more checks. Decide what the video adds before you start. If the episode is a conversation with no visual content, the extra effort may not pay off. If the guest demonstrates something, or the hosts react to each other visibly, video earns its place.

Think about the time as well as the money. A video episode can take several times as long to edit, because the cuts have to match the picture. A small team should test one video episode before committing to a schedule of them.

Keep the audio master first

Record audio as the foundation, even for video shows. A clean, lossless audio master can be used for the audio podcast, the video mix and the short clips. Recording video and audio on separate devices needs a sync point, so clap at the start of each take. The sync guide shows how to line them up, and the checks at the middle and end matter as much as the start.

Keep the same room and microphone position for video episodes as for audio ones. A show that sounds different when it becomes a video will lose its audio listeners, who are often the most loyal.

Plan the export for each platform

Export the audio version with a loudness target suited to podcast platforms, and export the video with its own level. Check both on a phone. The LUFS meter can check the final loudness of each file, so the two versions sound similar when people switch between them.

Name the files clearly, so the audio and video versions of the same episode can be found together. A simple pattern, with the date, the episode and the version, prevents confusion later.

A practical path

  1. Record the audio master first, in your usual room and level.
  2. Add a simple video setup with a fixed camera and good light.
  3. Sync the video to the audio using a clap, then check the middle and the end.
  4. Export the audio version and the video version separately, and check each on a phone.
  5. Review completion for the video episode against your audio episodes after a month.

Decide how the video episode is named and described

A video episode needs its own title and description, because platforms treat the video and audio versions differently in search. Use the same episode title in both places, and name the speakers in the description. Add a line that tells audio listeners the video exists, and a line that tells video viewers the audio is available too.

Keep the file names tidy as well. An episode with a clear name for each version is much easier to find later, when you want to make a clip or a compilation.

Limits

Not every show should go video. A show that works best as a private, intimate listen may lose something when the camera is on. The right choice depends on the content and the audience. Test one video episode, compare the completion data, and decide from the results rather than from fashion.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need video to grow a podcast?

Not always. Video helps some shows, but a strong audio show can grow without it.

Can I use the same recording for audio and video?

Yes, if you record audio as the master and sync the video to it.

What is the biggest mistake?

Treating the video as the main product and letting the audio slip. Viewers still judge the sound.

Do I need expensive cameras?

Not to start. Steady framing and good light matter more than the camera model.
