What Is Word Error Rate and How to Use It Without Being Misled

Updated October 2026 · Speech recognition and on-device transcription

Quick answer

Word error rate is the share of words in a transcript that are wrong, counting substitutions, deletions and insertions against a reference. It is useful for comparing tools on the same audio, but a single score hides which errors matter. Test on your own recordings and check the errors that affect meaning.

Word error rate, usually written as WER, is the most common way transcription accuracy is measured. It appears in research papers, product announcements and comparison charts. It is a useful measure, but it is easy to misread. A single percentage can hide the errors that matter most to you, and a tool that scores well on one kind of audio may do poorly on another.

This post explains what WER counts, why it can mislead, how to compare tools fairly and how to test a tool on your own material. It is written for creators, editors and anyone choosing a transcription tool.

How the measure works

WER compares a transcript with a reference transcript, which is a checked version of what was said. It counts the words that have been substituted, deleted or inserted, adds those up and divides by the number of words in the reference. A lower score means fewer errors. The measure is simple to calculate, which is one reason it is so widely used.

The result depends entirely on the reference. A careless reference, or one that treats spelling and punctuation differently, can change the score.

Why a single score misleads

A WER figure treats every error as equal. A missed filler word counts the same as a wrong name, a wrong number or a reversed negative. For a creator, the errors that change meaning are far more serious than the ones that do not. Two tools with the same WER may produce very different transcripts for your purposes.

The audio also matters. A score measured on clean studio speech may not hold for a noisy phone recording, a fast-talking panel or a speaker with a strong accent. Scores quoted by vendors often come from test sets that do not match your work.

Errors that change meaning

Some errors are more harmful than others. A negative that is dropped (“did not” becoming “did”) reverses a claim. A number that changes (“fifteen” becoming “fifty”) misleads. A name that is wrong damages trust. When you check a transcript, look first for these high-impact errors, not for the total score.

How to compare tools fairly

  1. Use the same audio for every tool, ideally a few minutes of your own recording.
  2. Create a careful reference transcript, checked by a person who listens closely.
  3. Count the errors by type, and note which ones change meaning.
  4. Compare the time you spend correcting each transcript, not only the score.
  5. Repeat the test on a second recording with different conditions, such as a noisier room.

What the score is good for

WER is useful for a rough comparison of tools on the same material, and for tracking whether a tool improves over time on your own audio. Use it as one measure among several. The time it takes to fix a transcript is often the most practical measure of all.

Why vendor scores need care

Vendors choose test sets, references and conditions that show their product well. A score from a vendor is a claim to be tested, not a fact to rely on. Ask what audio was used, how the reference was made and whether the test matches your work. An independent test on similar audio is more useful than any headline figure.

Use the score to track your own progress

A score is most useful over time, on your own audio. If you run the same short test each month, with the same recording and the same reference, you can see whether a tool, a setting or your own recording habits are improving the result. Keep the reference transcript and the test file in a folder, so the comparison stays honest.

A small change in the score is not always meaningful, especially on a short recording. Look for consistent movement across several tests before drawing a conclusion.

Explain the score to a non-specialist

When you report a transcription score to a client, a colleague or a funder, explain what it counts and what it does not. A plain statement, such as “about one word in twenty was wrong, and most of the errors were filler words”, is more honest and more useful than a percentage on its own. The people who read the report need the meaning, not only the number.

Limits

WER does not capture meaning, tone, timing or the usefulness of a transcript to a particular reader. Two transcripts with the same score can read very differently. Use WER as a starting point, and judge the transcript on what it does for your work.

Start with a clean recording

Frequently asked questions

Is a lower word error rate always better?

Usually, but only on similar audio. Check the errors that change meaning as well.

How do I calculate it myself?

Compare your transcript with a careful reference, count the substitutions, deletions and insertions, and divide by the reference words.

Why do vendor scores differ so much?

They use different test sets, references and conditions. Compare on your own audio.

Which errors matter most?

Errors that reverse a claim, change a number or get a name wrong, because they mislead the reader.
