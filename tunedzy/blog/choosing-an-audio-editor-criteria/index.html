How to Choose an Audio Editor: The Criteria That Matter Most

Updated October 2026 · Choosing audio tools

Quick answer

Choose an audio editor by the criteria that matter for your work: the type of editing you do, the file sizes, the export formats you need, how well it fits your workflow, how easy it is to learn, what it costs over time and how well it is supported. Decide the criteria first, test two or three options on the same project, and choose the one that makes your real work easier.

Audio editors range from simple tools to complex programs with many tracks and effects. The best one for you is the one that fits your work, not the one with the longest feature list or the most enthusiastic reviews. A short, criteria-based process helps you choose with confidence, and it prevents the common mistake of learning a tool that does not suit the job.

This post sets out the criteria that matter most, how to weigh them and how to test an editor before committing to it.

The type of editing you do

Start with the work. A spoken podcast needs cutting, levelling and simple processing. A music project needs multiple tracks, MIDI or sample handling and mixing tools. A video soundtrack needs syncing with the picture and managing many clips. Choose an editor that handles your main task well, and avoid paying for features you will never use.

File sizes and formats

Consider the length of your files and the formats you work with. A long, high-resolution recording needs an editor that handles large files smoothly. Check that the editor opens and exports the formats your platforms and collaborators need, and that it keeps the quality you require.

Workflow and fit

An editor should fit your routine. Look at how quickly you can move from import to export, how the timeline and the tools are laid out, and how easily you can find the features you use most. A good fit reduces friction, and a poor fit slows every session.

Learning curve

Consider how long it will take to become productive. A simple editor may be quick to learn but limited, while a powerful one may take weeks or months. Be realistic about the time you have, and choose a tool that you will actually learn. A tool you use well is better than a more capable one that you never master.

Export options and quality

Check the export settings: formats, sample rates, bit depth, loudness options and metadata. A good export keeps the quality of your master and gives you control over the final file. Test an export and listen to it on a phone before deciding.

Cost and continuity

Work out the total cost over the years you expect to use the editor, and check what happens to your projects if you stop paying or if the software changes. The subscription and one-time options are compared in a separate post in this series.

Support and community

Consider the documentation, the support and the community around the editor. Good documentation saves hours of frustration, and an active community can answer questions quickly. A tool with little support may be harder to use when something goes wrong.

Test before you commit

Test two or three editors on the same project. Import the same files, make the same edits, export the same result and compare the time and the quality. The test shows how the tool handles your real work, which is far more useful than a demonstration.

A simple decision method

  1. Write down your main type of editing and your file sizes.
  2. List the export formats and loudness options you need.
  3. Choose two or three editors that fit those needs.
  4. Test each on the same project, and time the work.
  5. Compare the exports by ear, and choose the best fit.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing by the longest feature list.
  • Choosing a tool that is too complex for the work.
  • Ignoring the export options until the project is finished.
  • Learning a tool without testing it on a real project.
  • Forgetting the total cost over several years.

Speed on your own machine

A demo that runs quickly on a sample file may slow down on a long project or an older computer. Test with the largest project you normally work with, and time the actions you repeat most often, such as opening, scrolling, zooming and exporting. A fast tool on your typical files is more useful than a fast tool on a sample.

Keep your projects portable

Choose a tool that can export a project, or its audio, in a standard format, so your work does not depend on a single application. Check how the project files are stored and whether the export keeps the levels, markers and fades you have set. Portable work is easier to hand over and easier to move when your needs change.

Limits

No editor is right for everyone, and needs change as your work grows. Re-assess your choice when your work changes, and accept that the best tool today may not be the best in a few years. This post offers a method for choosing, not a verdict on any product.

Frequently asked questions

What should I decide first?

The type of editing you do and the size of your files.

Should I choose the most powerful editor?

Not necessarily. Choose one you will learn and use well for your real work.

How do I test an editor?

Use the same project on two or three editors, time the work and compare the exports by ear.

How often should I re-check my choice?

When your work changes, or when your editor changes significantly.
