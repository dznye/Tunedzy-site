How to Make a Karaoke Backing Track at Home

Updated October 2026 · Singing at home

Quick answer

Start with a backing track you have the right to use: one you made, one you bought with a licence that allows it, or a track from a licensed library. Reduce the vocal if you own the full song, set the level so your voice sits clearly on top, and check the key before you sing.

A karaoke track lets you sing along to a song with the voice removed. It is a useful practice tool, and it is also a common source of rights questions. The safest route is a track you have the right to use. The technical steps are simple, but the licence comes first.

Check the rights first

Using a commercial recording with the vocal removed still uses the song. For private practice, the rules vary, and for anything you publish, you need a licence. Use a track you made, one from a library that allows your use, or a backing track sold for singing. Check the terms before you share a recording.

Remove the vocal, if you own the full song

Some tools can separate the vocal from a song, leaving an instrumental. The results vary, and separation often leaves artefacts, so listen carefully before you use the track. The stem separation guide explains how the process works and where it falls short.

Set the level and the mix

Set the backing track so you can hear it clearly, but keep it below your voice when you record. A backing track that is too loud makes you push, and it bleeds into the microphone. Test a short section, then adjust.

Check the key

A karaoke track is usually in the original key, which may not suit you. Use the Song Key Finder to check the key, then choose a version or a transposed track that sits in your range.

Limits

A home track rarely matches a professional karaoke version, and removed vocals often leave some trace of the original singer. For a performance or a published cover, use a track you have the licence for, and check the rules before you share it.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to make a karaoke track from any song?

Not automatically. The rights to the song and recording matter, so use tracks you have the right to use.

Can I remove the vocal from any song?

You can try with some tools, but the result may leave artefacts, and the rights still apply.

Why does the backing track sound muddy?

The separation left artefacts, or the mix is too busy. Try a cleaner source or a simpler track.

Should I sing along at the original key?

Only if it suits your range. Transposing is often better.
