AI in Podcast Production: What It Does Well and Where You Still Decide

Updated October 2026 · Podcast trends and what changes next

Quick answer

AI tools now handle transcription, first-pass cleanup, chapters and show notes well enough to save hours. They still make mistakes with names, numbers and tone, and they cannot decide what the episode is for. Use them for routine work and keep the creative and factual decisions yourself.

AI is now part of many podcast workflows. Tools can transcribe an episode, suggest chapters, remove some noise and draft show notes. The time saved is real. The mistakes are also real, and some of them matter. The producer still makes the decisions that shape the show, and understanding where those decisions sit keeps the work honest and the show trustworthy.

This post goes through the production steps in order, saying what the tools do well, where they fail, and where a person has to check or decide. It is written for producers who want to use the tools without handing over the show.

What AI does well

Routine tasks suit AI well. Transcription gets most of the words right on clear speech. Chapter suggestions save time when an episode has clear sections. First-pass noise reduction can clean up steady background sounds. Show note drafts give you a starting point. These are tasks where a quick pass saves time and a human check catches the rest.

The biggest gain is often in the first hour of work on each episode, when the producer is looking for the outline. A transcript and a set of suggested chapters let you see the shape of a long conversation quickly, which makes the editing decisions faster and better.

Where AI makes mistakes

Names, technical terms, numbers and dates are the most common errors. A wrong name in show notes is embarrassing, and a wrong number can mislead listeners. Speaker labels can also be wrong when two voices are similar. Accents, crosstalk and fast speech add more errors. Always check the facts against the audio, not against your memory of the conversation.

Tone is harder to check. A tool may tidy a joke into a flat sentence, or remove a hesitation that carried meaning. Listen to the cleaned audio for the moments that matter emotionally, because those are the parts a tool is least likely to understand.

What stays with the producer

Choosing what the episode is about, deciding what to cut, and judging whether the sound still feels like your show are human decisions. A tool can suggest a cut, but it does not know that a pause before a joke matters. It also cannot tell whether a guest would be upset by how they are edited. These judgments are the craft, and they are the reason listeners return.

Keep the editorial line in your hands. Decide the structure first, then use the tools to speed up the work inside it. A show built by a tool that chose its own structure will feel like it has no author.

Keep a human check on each step

  1. Check the transcript against the audio for names, numbers and quotes.
  2. Listen to the cleaned audio on headphones, especially the quiet parts and any emotional moments.
  3. Read the show notes and remove anything the episode did not say.
  4. Check the final loudness of the export with the LUFS meter, and listen on a phone.

Write these checks into your production checklist, so they happen every time, even when the tool seems to be doing well. The errors that matter are the ones you stop expecting.

Privacy and consent

Some AI tools send audio to a server, and some keep it for training. Read what each tool does with your files before you upload an interview, especially a private one. Tell guests if AI tools will process their voice, and agree how the files will be used. A guest who learns about a tool after the fact will not forgive the surprise.

For sensitive recordings, prefer tools that process audio on your own device, or check the policy carefully. The privacy post in this series sets out the questions to ask.

Document your workflow for guests and editors

When a tool processes part of the production, write that down in a short workflow note. Say which steps use AI, which use a person, and where each file is stored. Share the note with any editor, collaborator or guest who asks. Clear documentation builds trust, and it saves an awkward conversation when a question arises months later.

The note also helps you when you change tools. You can see which steps depend on the old tool and plan the switch without breaking the show.

Limits

AI tools change quickly, and the results vary by recording. Judge each tool on your own episodes, not on a general claim. A tool that works well on clear solo recordings may struggle with a noisy panel. Test before you commit to a workflow, and keep a manual route for the episodes that do not suit the tool.

Start with the basics

AI cleanup works best on a clean recording. Record in a quiet room, keep the microphone close, and check the level. Frequently asked questions

Will AI replace podcast editors?

It changes the routine parts of the job. The creative and factual decisions still need a person.

Can I trust the AI show notes?

Only after checking. Names, numbers and quotes need a human check against the audio.

Should I tell listeners I used AI?

Being open about it is a sensible habit, especially for voice work. Check the rules that apply to you.

Is AI cleanup safe for interviews?

It can be, if the tool does not keep or train on your audio, and you check the result carefully.
