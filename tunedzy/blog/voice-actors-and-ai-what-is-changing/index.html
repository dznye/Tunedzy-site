Voice Actors and AI: What Is Changing and What to Do About It

Updated October 2026 · Voice rights, consent and deepfake labels

Quick answer

AI is changing voice work, with some routine jobs automated and new questions about consent, pay and reuse of voices. Voice actors and producers can respond with clear contracts that define each use, fair pay for any reuse and written consent for any generated voice. Responsible producers label generated speech.

Voice acting has always been shaped by technology, from recording studios to remote sessions. AI now raises a sharper question: can a voice be captured once and reused in ways the performer never agreed? That question is changing contracts, expectations and the way producers plan their work. This post explains the changes, the questions they raise and the steps that make the work fairer for everyone.

It draws on the general direction of the industry and on public debate, rather than on any single report. Where a specific claim depends on a particular case or a law, it is marked as such, and the reader should check the current position.

What is changing

Some routine voice tasks, such as simple narration, reading of short prompts or basic dubbing, are being automated in some projects. Producers may use generated voices for drafts, for prototypes or for lower-budget content. Voice actors report being asked to record a session that could later be used to train or generate a model, which raises questions about what the performance is worth.

At the same time, new work is appearing. Some voice actors license their voices for specific uses, and some producers use generated speech for accessibility or for versions in other languages. The change is not only a loss, but a shift in what a voice is licensed to do.

The contract question

The most important question is the contract. A voice actor should know what the recording may be used for, whether it can be used to build a model, for how long, in which territories and for which fees. A vague clause that allows any future use is a risk. A clear clause that limits the use, and requires a new agreement for anything else, protects the performer.

Producers who want a generated version of a performer’s voice should ask for a separate agreement, and pay fairly for it. Reuse is a use, and it should be priced as one.

Consent and fair pay

Consent should be specific and revocable where possible. A performer should be able to see how their voice is used and to stop future use where the contract allows. Fair pay for reuse means that a performance is not treated as a one-off fee when it is used again and again. The details vary by contract, but the principle is that the performer shares in the value they help create.

What producers can do

  1. Write contracts that name each permitted use, and require a new agreement for anything else.
  2. Pay separately for any model training or generated voice, and say so in the contract.
  3. Label generated speech clearly in the finished work, as covered in the labelling guide.
  4. Keep records of consent, sessions and payments, and share them with the performer on request.
  5. Offer performers a way to decline a generated use without losing other work.

What voice actors can do

A performer can ask for a clear contract before a session, read the clauses on reuse and training, and keep a copy of what was agreed. They can negotiate a limit on the uses, ask for a fee for each new use, and say no to a session that seems designed for a model rather than a project. Joining a professional body or union can help with shared standards and advice.

Building a fair working relationship

A fair relationship between a producer and a voice actor starts before the session. Explain the project, the expected uses and the timetable. Ask what the performer needs, and listen to their concerns about reuse. A performer who understands the plan is more likely to give a strong, committed performance.

Keep the relationship open after the session. Tell the performer when the work is used, share the finished version, and pay promptly. Trust built this way makes future work easier for both sides.

Plan for the long term

A voice actor’s career depends on repeat work and a reputation for reliability. A contract that trades a long-term future for a single fee may look attractive now but can damage that career. Before signing, think about how the recording could be used in five years, and whether the fee reflects that use. A producer who asks for a fair long-term deal often finds it easier to hire the same performer again.

For producers, the long view means building a library of consented voices with clear terms. A well-documented library is easier to manage, easier to audit and more valuable than a collection of recordings with unclear rights.

Limits

The industry is changing quickly, and practice varies by country, by union and by sector. This post describes the main questions rather than the rules in any one market. Anyone signing a contract should take advice from a professional who knows the local position.

Frequently asked questions

Will AI replace voice actors?

It is changing some routine work. Demand for clear, skilled performance remains, but contracts and pay are being reshaped.

What should a voice contract say?

Each permitted use, the territory, the duration, any training or model use, and the fee for each use.

Can a producer reuse a recording later?

Only where the contract allows it. A new use needs a new agreement, and usually new payment.

Should generated speech be labelled?

Yes, where it could be taken as real. Labelling is good practice and in some places a legal duty.
