How to Clean a Noisy Interview Recording Without Ruining It

Updated October 2026 · Cleaning speech recordings

Quick answer

Clean a noisy interview in a fixed order: assess the damage first, fix equipment faults, then reduce steady noise gently, then balance the voices and finally check the result. Over-cleaning makes the guest sound distant or watery, so keep each step light, and accept that a very noisy recording may not be worth saving.

An interview recorded in a noisy place is a common problem. A café, a street, a busy office or a room with a fan can bring so much background into the recording that the guest is hard to hear. The temptation is to push the cleanup hard, and that often damages the voice more than the noise. A careful approach, in the right order, can save many recordings that a heavy-handed edit would ruin.

This post shows how to assess the damage, the order of the fixes, how to reduce noise gently and how to decide when a recording is not worth saving.

Assess the damage first

Before any cleaning, listen to the whole interview on headphones, and note the problems. Is the noise steady or sudden? Are the voices clear or muffled? Is the level even, or does it drop when the guest turns away? Are there clicks, dropouts or clipping? Write each problem down with its time, so you know what to fix and in what order.

The assessment also tells you whether the recording is worth saving. If the guest is hard to understand for long stretches, the time spent may not be justified.

Fix the equipment faults first

Clicks, dropouts and hum usually come from equipment or connections. Repair them before any noise reduction, because a noise reducer will spread a click into a smear, and it cannot remove a dropout. Use the targeted methods for each fault, such as cutting a click or filling a dropout with room tone.

Reduce steady noise gently

Steady noise, such as a fan or traffic hum, can be reduced with a noise reducer. Capture a short sample of the noise alone, if you have one, and apply a light reduction to the interview. Listen to the quiet pauses and the guest’s consonants. If the voice becomes thin or watery, reduce the amount, or accept more noise rather than a damaged voice.

Balance the voices

An interview in a noisy place often has a guest who was far from the microphone and a host who was close. Balance the two so they sit together, using gain changes on each voice. Keep the guest’s natural variation, and avoid lifting the quiet passages so far that the room noise rises with them.

Check the result

Listen to the finished interview on headphones, then on a phone speaker, at a normal volume. Check that the guest can be understood throughout, that the noise is controlled and that the voices are balanced. The LUFS meter can check the loudness of the export against your target.

When to re-record the questions

If a key answer is covered by noise, consider asking the guest to answer it again, perhaps in a quieter setting. A short re-recorded answer, clearly marked for the edit, is often better than a long repair. Be open with the guest about why you are asking, and keep the re-recording short.

When the recording is not worth saving

Some recordings are too damaged to save. If the guest is hard to hear for most of the interview, or if a vital part is lost to noise, a short summary or a written follow-up may be a better outcome. Decide early, and tell the guest what you plan to do.

Keep the guest comfortable through the edit

A guest who sounds poor on a recording may feel embarrassed, even when the problem came from the room. Explain what you plan to do before you send the edit, and let them hear the version you intend to publish. A guest who understands the process is more forgiving of the limits, and more likely to agree to a short re-record of a key answer.

Keep the guest’s approval in writing if you change the content in any significant way, such as cutting an answer or moving sections. A short email confirming the agreed version protects both of you.

Keep the cleanup of each section separate, so a mistake in one part does not spread through the whole interview. Process the questions and answers in order, listening to each before moving on, and save a copy of each stage. If a later change damages the sound, you can return to the last good stage rather than starting from the raw recording again.

Limits

No tool recovers speech that noise has covered. The best cleanup keeps the voice natural and removes only what distracts. A recording made in the right place will always be easier to clean than one made in a noisy one.

Frequently asked questions

Should I clean the noise first or the clicks first?

Clicks and faults first, then steady noise, so the noise reducer does not smear them.

How much noise reduction is too much?

When the guest sounds thin, watery or distant. Reduce the amount and accept some noise.

Can I ask the guest to re-answer?

Yes, in a quieter setting, and keep it short. Tell the guest why you are asking.

When should I give up on a recording?

When a large part is lost to noise and no repair will make it understandable.
