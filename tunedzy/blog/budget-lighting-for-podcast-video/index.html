Budget Lighting for Podcast Video: Simple Setups That Work

Updated October 2026 · Video podcasts and audio-first shows

Quick answer

Good podcast video needs soft, even light on the faces. A window with a sheer curtain, a soft lamp at the side and a white reflector on the other side cover most home setups. Keep the light steady for the whole episode, and avoid mixing daylight with a warm lamp in the same frame.

Lighting is the part of a video podcast that viewers notice most and creators fix least. A dim, uneven face makes an episode feel low quality, even when the sound is excellent. The good news is that a simple, cheap setup can look professional if it is soft, steady and placed with care. This guide covers the basics for a home studio.

The aim is not dramatic lighting. The aim is a face that is clear, a background that is not distracting and a look that stays the same across episodes. Once you have that, the rest of the production is easier.

Use a window first

Daylight from a window is the best free light most people have. Place the subject at an angle to the window, so the light falls across the face rather than straight on or from behind. A sheer curtain softens the light and reduces harsh shadows. Film at the same time of day if you can, because daylight changes quickly and can make two episodes look different.

Avoid placing the subject with the window directly behind them. The face will be dark, and the camera will try to compensate with noise and a washed-out background.

Add a soft lamp

When daylight is not enough, a soft lamp at the side can fill the shadows. A lamp with a diffuser, or a lamp pointed at a white wall, gives softer light than a bare bulb. Place it a little above eye level and to one side, and keep it at a similar distance each time. The aim is to add light, not to create a new shadow.

Mixing light sources is a common problem. Daylight is cool and a warm lamp is orange, and the two can make skin look strange. Either switch off the room light and use only one source, or set the camera’s white balance to match what is on.

Use a reflector

A white card, a foam board or even a sheet of paper can bounce light back into the shadow side of the face. Place it on the side opposite the main light, and move it until the shadow softens. A reflector is cheap and one of the most effective tools for a small setup.

Control the background

A plain, slightly out-of-focus background is easiest to live with. Keep clutter out of the frame, and avoid a bright window behind the subject. A bookshelf or a plain wall works well. If the background is busy, move the chair or the camera rather than adding more lights.

Keep the setup steady

Mark the position of the chairs, the camera and the lamp on the floor with tape. Note the camera settings in a short template. A steady setup makes every episode look like part of the same show, and it saves time before each recording.

Check the result on a phone

Watch a short test on a phone. Check that the faces are clear, the shadows are soft and the background is calm. Adjust one thing at a time, and check again. Most viewers watch on a phone, so that is the screen that matters most.

Common mistakes

  • Placing the subject in front of a bright window, which leaves the face dark.
  • Using a single harsh light close to the face, which creates strong shadows.
  • Mixing daylight and warm lamp light in one frame.
  • Letting the light change during an episode, so the face gets brighter or darker halfway through.

Work out the light for each seat

In a two-person shot, each face needs its own light. A single window may light one person well and leave the other in shadow. Sit each person in the position they will use for the episode, then check their face on screen. If one face is much darker, move the reflector to their side or adjust the lamp, then check again.

Pay attention to hair and glasses too. A light placed high and to the side can throw glare onto lenses, and a light directly above can make hair look flat. Small changes in angle often solve these problems without any new equipment.

Plan for changing daylight

Daylight changes through the afternoon, and a session that starts in bright sun may end in dusk. Decide on a time of day for recording and stick to it, so every episode looks similar. If you must film at different times, add a soft lamp that you can use to match the daylight, and check the white balance before the first take.

Clouds can change the light in minutes. Keep the lamp on hand, and treat a sudden change as a reason to pause and re-check the frame rather than pressing on.

Limits

A budget setup will not match a studio. It will work well for a conversation show, but a complex scene needs more equipment and more time. Lighting cannot fix a poor camera or a cluttered room. Start with what you have, and improve one part at a time.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use only a window?

Yes, if the window is at an angle to the subject and you film at a steady time of day.

What is the cheapest improvement?

A reflector on the shadow side of the face. It costs very little and makes a visible difference.

Do I need a special light?

Not to start. A soft lamp with a diffuser is enough for most small setups.

Why does my face look orange?

Warm lamp light is mixed with daylight or a cool camera setting. Use one light source or fix the white balance.
