Field Recording for Beginners: Capturing Sound Where It Happens

Updated October 2026 · Field recording and nature sounds

Quick answer

Field recording captures sound where it happens, outdoors or in real locations, for use in video, music, sound libraries and personal projects. Beginners need a portable recorder, a windscreen, headphones and a habit of listening before pressing record. Choose the location carefully, control wind and handling noise, and record generously so there is material to choose from.

Field recording is the practice of capturing sound at the place where it happens. It might be a street market, a forest after rain, a workshop, a train station or a quiet morning in a garden. The recordings can be used in videos, music, sound design, documentary work or simply as a personal archive of places. The practice is accessible, and a beginner can start with a modest recorder and a pair of headphones.

This post introduces the basics: what field recording involves, the kit that matters most, how to choose a location, and the habits that produce clean, usable sound outdoors.

What field recording is

A field recording is a sound captured in its natural setting, without the controlled conditions of a studio. The sound carries the character of the place: its size, its activity and its weather. Because the environment is uncontrolled, field recording is partly a matter of patience, timing and careful listening, as much as technical skill.

The results can be surprisingly rich. A recording of a busy street, a rainstorm or a crackling fire can carry more atmosphere than a generated sound, because it is the real thing.

The basic kit

A portable recorder with a good microphone is the core of the kit. A pair of closed-back headphones lets you hear what you are capturing, and a windscreen is essential for almost every outdoor recording. Spare batteries or a power bank, and enough storage for long sessions, complete the basics. You do not need the most expensive equipment to start. A reliable, simple recorder used well will produce more useful sound than an elaborate setup used carelessly.

Choose the location

Location decides most of what you capture. Look for places where the sound you want is clear and the sound you do not want is limited. A park far from roads is better for birds than a park next to a main road. A harbour at dawn may be quieter than at midday. Visit the location before recording, listen at different times, and note the sounds that come and go.

Control the wind

Wind is the most common problem in field recording. Even a light breeze can roar across the microphone and bury the sound you came for. Use a windscreen, and for strong wind a furry cover. Shelter the microphone behind a bush, a wall or your own body, and keep it low to the ground if the wind is coming from above. A recording that is spoiled by wind cannot usually be repaired.

Control handling noise

Handling noise comes from touching the recorder or its cable. Use a stand, a mount or a shock mount where possible, and keep your hands off the recorder during the take. Set the recorder down on a soft surface, and keep the cable slack so it does not tug. Many beginners lose good material to a bump they did not notice.

Listen before you record

Stand still for a minute before recording, and listen. Note the sounds that dominate, the sounds that come and go and the sounds that are background. Move the microphone a little, and listen again. The best position is often a few metres from where you first stood, and listening is how you find it.

Record generously

Record longer than you think you need. A field session can produce a few minutes of usable material from an hour of recording, and the extra time lets you capture moments you did not expect. Start each recording with a few seconds of silence, and note the time, the place and the conditions.

Common mistakes

  • Recording without a windscreen, so the wind dominates.
  • Touching the recorder during a take, so handling noise appears.
  • Recording in the middle of the busiest moment, and missing the quiet.
  • Forgetting to note the place, the time and the conditions.
  • Stopping too early, and missing the best moment of the session.

A first outing

  1. Choose one place near home that has interesting sound.
  2. Visit at two different times, and listen before recording.
  3. Record for twenty minutes, with a windscreen and headphones.
  4. Label the files with the place, the time and the conditions.
  5. Listen back, and note what you would change next time.

Respect the place and the people

Field recording often takes place in public, and it can involve other people and animals. Respect their privacy, avoid recording identifiable people without consent when you plan to publish, and do not disturb wildlife or sensitive habitats. Some locations have rules about recording, and some private spaces need permission. Check before you go.

Limits

Field recording depends on weather, season and luck. A session may be ruined by an unexpected plane or rain, and that is part of the practice. The aim is to learn to listen, to plan and to accept what the environment offers. Expect many recordings you will not use, and treat each one as practice.

Frequently asked questions

What do I need to start?

A portable recorder, a windscreen, headphones and a few spare batteries and storage cards.

What is the biggest problem outdoors?

Wind. A windscreen and sheltered positions prevent most of it.

How long should I record?

Longer than you think. Record generously, since many minutes of a session may be unusable.

Do I need permission to record?

Sometimes. Check the rules for the location and respect the people and animals there.
