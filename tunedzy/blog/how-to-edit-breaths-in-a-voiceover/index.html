How to Edit Breaths in a Voiceover Without Losing the Voice

Updated October 2026 · Voiceover and narration

Quick answer

Keep most breaths, because they make a voice sound human. Shorten the loud or long ones that distract, and remove a breath only when it sits in the wrong place. Use a small fade or a crossfade rather than a hard cut, so the edit does not jump.

Breaths are part of speech, and a voiceover with every breath removed sounds unnatural and tired in a different way. Listeners notice when a voice never seems to breathe. The job is to make the breaths quieter and better placed, not to remove them all.

Why keep most breaths

A breath tells the listener that a person is speaking, not a machine. It also marks a pause between ideas, which helps the listener follow. Removing every breath can make a narration sound rushed, with no room to think. Keep the ones that sound natural at normal volume.

Shorten the loud or long ones

Breaths that are much louder than the speech, or that last for a long time, distract the listener. Lower them by a few decibels, or shorten them so they sound like a quick intake. Make small changes, and listen at normal volume after each one.

Remove a breath only when it is in the wrong place

A breath in the middle of a short phrase, or one that cuts a word in two, should be removed. Do it carefully, by cutting in a quiet part of the breath and keeping the room tone behind it. A breath that starts a new sentence is usually fine to keep.

Make the joins smooth

A hard cut across a breath can make the voice jump. Use a short fade or a crossfade of a few milliseconds, and keep a little room tone under the join. The crossfade guide shows the method, which works the same way for breath edits.

Compare with the original

After editing, play the edited section next to the original. If the edit sounds cleaner but less alive, you have removed too much. The best edit is the one that a listener cannot point to, because nothing sounds wrong.

Limits

Breath editing takes time, and it is easy to over-do. For a short voiceover, a few careful edits are enough. For long-form narration, consider a lighter approach, so the sound stays consistent across the whole recording.

Frequently asked questions

Should I remove all breaths from a voiceover?

No. Keep most of them, because they make the voice sound human.

How do I make a breath quieter?

Lower its level a few decibels, or shorten it so it sounds like a quick intake.

Will listeners notice a breath edit?

Not if the join is smooth and the room tone matches. A hard cut is the most noticeable part.

Should I edit breaths before or after cleaning the file?

Clean the file first, then edit the breaths, so the room noise is consistent when you make the changes.
