Recording and Gaming in One Room: Making a Shared Space Work

Updated October 2026 · Planning a home studio

Quick answer

Recording and gaming in one room is possible with planning. Keep the computer and its fans away from the microphone, set the game and music sound below the voice, use headphones to avoid bleed and schedule recording sessions when the gaming is off. A clear routine lets the same room serve both purposes without damaging the recordings.

Many home creators share a room between recording and gaming. The desk that holds the microphone is also the desk that holds the game, and the sound of the game can easily bleed into a voice recording. A shared room can work well with a clear routine, sensible placement and some discipline about when each task happens. This post explains how to make the arrangement work for both.

The main issues are the computer noise, the sound of the game, the bleed into the microphone and the scheduling of sessions, and each has a simple solution.

Keep the computer quiet and away

A gaming computer can be loud, with fans and drives that reach the microphone. Move the computer as far from the microphone as the cables allow, and place it on a soft mat if it vibrates. Choose a quieter fan setting if the computer allows it, and keep the case closed. A quieter machine makes every recording easier.

Use headphones for the game

Headphones keep the game out of the microphone. Speakers project the game into the room, and the microphone picks it up. Use closed-back headphones for gaming during any session where you are recording, and check that the game sound does not leak into the microphone.

Balance the game sound

If you record while playing, or if the game is part of the content, set the game and music below the voice in the recording chain. A loud game can mask the voice, and a quiet voice next to a loud game is hard to follow. Use the same balance you would use for a stream, as described in the stream levels post in this series.

Schedule the sessions

The simplest solution is to schedule recording and gaming at different times. Record when the computer is quiet and the game is off, and game when the room is free. A shared calendar or a simple note on the door helps everyone in the household know when the room is in use.

Protect the recording setup

Keep the microphone, its stand and its cable out of the gaming area, so they are not knocked or moved during play. Mark the microphone position, and cover the equipment when it is not in use. A recording setup that is left in place is more consistent than one that is moved for every session.

Test the room for each mode

Record a short test in each mode: gaming with the headphones on, and recording with the game off. Listen for the bleed, the computer noise and the balance. A test before each important session saves a lot of rework later.

Common mistakes

  • Using speakers for the game during a recording.
  • Leaving the computer close to the microphone.
  • Letting the game music play during a voice-over.
  • Moving the microphone each time the room changes use.
  • Recording without checking the room in its current mode.

Choose a microphone for a shared room

A close, cardioid microphone picks up less of the room and the game than an open pattern does, which helps in a shared space. Keep it close to the mouth, and position it so the voice is louder at the microphone than the computer's noise and the game's sound. A simple test with the game running will show quickly whether the setup holds up.

Clean up after each session

Save the session settings, note the game audio levels and close the windows that were open during the recording. A clear end to each session keeps the next recording consistent, and it stops the room from slowly reverting to its gaming setup. If the room is shared with other people, agree on the same routine with them so the handover is clean.

Agreeing the rules with others

If other people share the room or the computer, agree the rules in writing: when recording happens, how the game audio is handled, who clears the desk and who checks the levels. A short written routine avoids arguments and keeps the room usable for both purposes. A visible note near the desk helps everyone follow the plan. Keep the note short and specific, and review it if the schedule or the equipment changes.

Limits

A shared room will never match a dedicated studio, and some sessions will suffer from the noise of the other activity. The aim is to make the room work well enough for the recordings you need. Where the conflict is serious, a separate space, even a small one, may be the better answer over time.

Frequently asked questions

Can I record and game in the same room?

Yes, with planning: quiet computer, headphones and scheduled sessions.

How do I stop the game bleeding into the microphone?

Use closed-back headphones, not speakers, during any recording session.

What is the simplest solution?

Schedule recording and gaming at different times, so the room is quiet for each session.

Should I check the room before recording?

Yes. A short test in the current mode shows any bleed or noise before it spoils a session.
