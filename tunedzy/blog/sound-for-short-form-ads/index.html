Sound for Short-Form Ads: Getting Attention in the First Second

Updated October 2026 · Sound design and sound effects

Quick answer

Sound for a short-form ad must grab attention quickly and keep the message clear on small speakers. Open with a clear voice or a strong sound in the first second, keep the music under the message, use effects sparingly to emphasise the offer, and check the result with the sound off and on, on a phone.

A short-form ad has only seconds to win the viewer’s attention. Many people watch with the sound off, and many who listen do so on a phone speaker in a noisy place. The sound must work in those conditions, and it must support the message rather than compete with it. A good ad uses sound to make the first second count, to carry the offer clearly and to leave a memorable impression.

This post explains how to use sound in short-form advertising, from the opening second to the final call to action, with the checks that keep the message clear.

Open with purpose

The first second decides whether many viewers stay. A strong opening can be a clear voice, a distinctive sound or a surprising moment, but it must be purposeful. A sound that is pleasant but meaningless wastes the moment. Open with the element that tells the viewer what the ad is about, and let the sound support that message.

Keep the voice clear

In most short ads, the voice carries the message. It must be clear, close and easy to understand on a phone speaker. Record it in a quiet room with the microphone close, and check the level so the words are not lost under music or effects. Subtitles help viewers who watch with the sound off, but the voice must still work on its own.

Music under the message

Music in a short ad sets the mood and the pace, but it must sit under the voice. Choose a track with a clear rhythm and little vocal, and duck it when the message is spoken. Make sure the music is licensed for advertising, since advertising uses are often restricted more than general content use. The music under talking post covers the balance.

Use effects to emphasise the offer

A well-placed effect can draw attention to the offer, such as a short chime when a price appears or a swoosh when a product arrives. Use effects sparingly, and place them exactly where the message needs emphasis. Too many effects make the ad feel busy and reduce the impact of each one.

Plan for the sound off

Many viewers watch without sound, so the message must work visually as well. Use captions for the key words, and make sure the picture alone tells the story. The sound then adds to the experience rather than being the only way the message gets through.

End with a clear call to action

The end of the ad is where the call to action lands. Use a clear voice, a short phrase and perhaps a final sound that signals the end. Keep the final moment simple and memorable, so the viewer knows what to do next. A rushed ending or a sudden cut can lose the action the ad was built to deliver.

Check on a phone with the sound off and on

Test the ad on a phone in both conditions: sound off, to check the picture and captions, and sound on, to check the voice and the balance. Listen in a noisy place if you can. An ad that works only in a quiet studio is not ready.

Common mistakes

  • Opening with a sound that is pleasant but carries no message.
  • Letting music mask the voice or the offer.
  • Using too many effects, so none stands out.
  • Forgetting that many viewers watch with the sound off.
  • Ending abruptly, so the call to action is lost.

Testing the first two seconds

In short advertising the opening moment decides whether the viewer stays. Play the first two seconds to a friend who has not seen the ad, and ask what they heard and what they expected. If the answer is vague, the opening sound needs a clearer role, such as a strong voice line, a recognisable effect or a visual cue with a matching sound.

Keeping the brand sound consistent

If the ad uses a sonic logo or a familiar voice, keep the level and tone close to the rest of the brand's audio. A sudden change can feel like a different company, even when the message is the same. Check the ad alongside other material from the brand before it goes out.

Limits

Advertising rules differ by country and sector, and claims in an ad must be accurate and fair. Sound can make a claim feel stronger than the facts support, so check every line of the script against the rules that apply. This post covers the craft of sound, not the legal approval of the content. For an ad with significant spend, take advice on the rules.

Frequently asked questions

What matters most in the first second?

A sound or voice that tells the viewer what the ad is about, placed purposefully.

Should the music be loud?

No. It should sit under the voice and support the mood without masking the message.

How do I reach viewers with the sound off?

Use captions for key words and make sure the picture alone tells the story.

Can I use any track in an ad?

No. Advertising uses are often restricted, so check the licence for advertising specifically.
