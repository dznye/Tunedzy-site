The UK Voice Rights Gap: What Commentators Say It Means

Updated October 2026 · Voice rights, consent and deepfake labels

Quick answer

A July 2026 analysis by the law firm CMS argues that UK law does not clearly protect people from unauthorised AI cloning of their voice. The protections that exist come from several areas not designed for the purpose. Creators in the UK should use written consent and label generated voices while the law is unsettled.

In June 2026, a news report raised concern that UK law does not protect people from unauthorised AI cloning of their voice or face. A July 2026 analysis from the law firm CMS took up the question and concluded that the existing legislation predates the technology and is not clearly fit for the purpose. This post explains what that analysis says, what it does not say, and what a UK creator can do while the position is debated.

The post relies on the commentary and on the report it describes. It is not a statement of the law, and anyone with a specific question should take advice from a professional who knows the current position.

What the analysis says

According to the analysis, there is no general right in UK law to own one’s voice in the way that some people might expect. Protection instead comes from several separate areas. Copyright can protect a recording, data protection can cover personal information including some voice data, trademarks can protect a name or brand, and the tort of passing off can apply where a person’s reputation is used to sell something without permission.

The analysis argues that none of these was designed with AI cloning in mind, so each has gaps. A cloned voice may not be a copy of an existing recording, and a voice may not be personal data in every case. That is the heart of the argument.

What it does not say

The analysis does not say that cloning a voice is always lawful, and it does not describe the outcome of any case. It is a commentary on the existing framework, prompted by a news report. Claims that the law has already changed, or that a particular court has ruled, should be checked against the primary sources, because not every claim in circulation has been verified.

Legislation may change, and the government may respond. Check the current position on the official legislation website, and watch for any consultation or bill that touches on digital replicas or personal data.

Why creators should care

A UK creator who clones a voice, or who works with a person whose voice is cloned, may face a dispute even where the law is unclear. A dispute can be costly and damaging, even if the outcome is uncertain. The safer course is to rely on consent, a clear agreement and honest labelling, rather than on a reading of unsettled law.

The same applies to the speakers. A podcast guest whose voice might be copied has an interest in knowing how the recording will be used and kept. Agreeing that in writing protects both sides.

Practical steps while the law is debated

  1. Get written consent before recording or generating any voice, stating the intended uses.
  2. Set a time limit on how long the voice files are kept, and delete them when it expires.
  3. Label any generated or altered voice clearly in the content.
  4. Keep the raw recordings and the consent note together, with a dated record.
  5. Take advice before any commercial use, especially in advertising or sponsorship.

What to watch

Watch for changes in legislation, consultations on digital replicas and any guidance from the bodies that regulate data and advertising. Each of these could change the practical position. Keep a short note of the sources you rely on and check them again before a major project.

What a UK creator should document

If you record or generate a voice in the UK, keep a short record that answers four questions: who the voice belongs to, what they agreed, what it will be used for, and when it will be deleted. A dated copy of the consent, the session notes and any licence should sit with the project files.

Document the tools too. Note which tool generated or altered the voice, what the terms said at the time, and whether the audio was uploaded. That record helps if a question arises about how the voice was made.

Read the position for the country your audience is in

A creator based in one country may publish to listeners in another, and the rules that apply can follow the audience as well as the speaker. A UK creator with a large audience abroad should check the rules in those places too, because a clip that is lawful at home may breach a different regime elsewhere. Map your main audiences before you publish, and note which rules you have checked for each.

When the position is unclear, the cautious choice is the one most creators would accept on reflection: consent in writing, a clear label and no use that could mislead. Those three habits satisfy most sensible readings of the law as it stands, while the debate continues.

Limits

This post summarises commentary and a news report, and it has not checked the underlying legal texts line by line. The analysis itself is a single view, and other lawyers may read the position differently. It is general guidance and not legal advice.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a UK law that protects my voice?

Commentators say protection comes from several separate areas, not one right. Check the current position with a professional.

Can a cloned voice be copyright?

Copyright may protect a recording, but not always a generated voice. The position is unsettled.

What should I do as a UK creator?

Get written consent, label generated voices, and take advice before any commercial use.

Where do I check the latest position?

On the official legislation website and the websites of the regulators, not only in summaries.
