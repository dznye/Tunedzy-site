Recording Rain and Water Sounds: Capturing Weather and Movement

Updated October 2026 · Field recording and nature sounds

Quick answer

Recording rain and water sounds rewards attention to surface, distance and wind. Rain sounds differ on leaves, roofs, pavements and water, and each needs a different placement. Use a windscreen, keep the microphone sheltered from splash, and record long enough to capture the changing intensity of the weather.

Rain and water sounds are among the most calming and atmospheric recordings a creator can make. A soft rain on leaves, a heavy downpour on a metal roof, a stream over stones or waves on a shore all carry character and mood. The sounds are also surprisingly varied, because the surface, the distance and the intensity of the water change the sound dramatically.

This post explains how to place the microphone for different kinds of rain and water, how to handle wind and splash and how to record for long enough to capture the full range of the weather.

Rain on different surfaces

Rain sounds differ greatly with the surface it falls on. Leaves produce a soft, broken patter, a roof produces a steady drum, a pavement produces a sharper hiss, and water produces a bright, gentle splash. Choose the surface you want, and place the microphone to capture it. Record several surfaces in the same storm, if you can, to hear the contrast.

Place the microphone

Place the microphone a little away from the main surface, so the sound is balanced rather than dominated by one drip or puddle. Angle it toward the rain, and keep it under cover if possible, since heavy drops on the microphone itself can be very loud and unpleasant. Test the position on headphones before the rain intensifies.

Handle wind and splash

Wind is the most common problem with outdoor rain recordings, as it can roar across the microphone. Use a windscreen, and shelter the microphone behind a wall or an overhang. Splash from nearby puddles and water can also be too loud, so keep the microphone above the splash line and away from direct spray.

Water sounds: streams and waves

Streams produce a continuous, changing sound that is pleasant to record. Place the microphone close enough to hear the water clearly, but not so close that it is overwhelmed by splash. Waves on a shore vary in rhythm, and a recording of several minutes captures the variation. Record away from the surf line, to avoid the strongest spray.

Set the level

Rain and water can be steady and then suddenly loud, as a gust or a large wave passes. Set the level with a short test, leaving room for the loudest moment. A sudden peak that clips cannot be repaired, so headroom matters even for steady-looking sounds.

Record for long periods

The weather changes, and so does the sound. A rainstorm may begin softly, build to a peak and ease off. Record for long enough to capture that arc, and for a long stream or sea recording, record for many minutes to capture the variation. Check the battery and the storage, since long recordings use both.

Common mistakes

  • Placing the microphone under a single drip, so one sound dominates.
  • Recording without a windscreen, so the wind overwhelms the rain.
  • Letting splash hit the microphone directly.
  • Recording only briefly, and missing the changing intensity of the weather.
  • Setting the level by a quiet moment, then clipping on a heavy downpour.

A simple plan

  1. Choose the surface or the water you want to capture.
  2. Place the microphone sheltered and away from the splash line.
  3. Use a windscreen, and set the level with a test.
  4. Record for a long period, to capture the changes in intensity.
  5. Label the file with the weather, the surface and the time.

Record the quiet between the drops

Rain can be loud, and it is easy to set the level for the peak and lose the quiet between the sounds. Listen for the spaces, since they carry the sense of distance and the air around the recording. A level that keeps the gaps audible usually makes a better recording than one that captures only the loudest moments.

Protect the gear in wet weather

A cover or bag that lets the microphone breathe will keep water out without trapping moisture around the capsule. Wipe the equipment after each session, and dry the windshield before storing it. Water left on a microphone can cause faults that are difficult to repair later.

Keeping the level steady over time

Long rain recordings can drift if a gust of wind or a change in the downpour shifts the level. Set the gain before the recording starts, and avoid touching it during the session. If you must adjust it, note the time, so you can see where the change happens during editing and decide whether it matters.

Limits

Weather is unpredictable, and a planned recording may be spoiled by a sudden gust or a change in the rain. Some storms are dangerous, and recording in them should never put you or your equipment at risk. Choose safe, sheltered positions, and accept that the weather decides the outcome.

Frequently asked questions

Which surface makes the best rain sound?

It depends on the mood. Leaves are soft, roofs are steady, and pavements are sharper.

How do I stop the wind spoiling rain recordings?

Use a windscreen and shelter the microphone behind a wall or overhang.

How long should I record rain?

Long enough to capture its changing intensity, often many minutes.

Is it safe to record in a storm?

Only from a safe, sheltered position. Never put yourself or your equipment at risk.
